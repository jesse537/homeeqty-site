HELOC or 401k loan: which is the better way to borrow?

Written by Home Equity Lending. Last updated November 17, 2026.

A HELOC borrows against your home, while a 401k loan borrows from your own retirement savings and is repaid through your plan.

A 401k loan does not put your home at risk, but it pulls money out of your retirement investments, is generally limited to the lesser of 50,000 dollars or half your vested balance, and your plan may require full repayment if you leave your job.

Questions to ask yourself

  • Is this a smaller, short term need, and is your job steady enough to repay a 401k loan on schedule?
  • Is this a larger or ongoing need, and do you want to leave your retirement savings invested?
  • If you are near retirement, could you keep up a HELOC payment after you retire, and how much would a 401k loan reduce what you have invested?

Risks in plain language

  • With a HELOC, your home is the collateral. If you cannot repay, you could lose your home.
  • If you use a HELOC to pay off credit cards or other unsecured debt, that debt becomes secured by your home.
  • HELOC payments can change and can jump when the repayment period starts.
  • A 401k loan you do not repay on schedule is generally taxed as a distribution and may also face the 10 percent early distribution tax.
  • Money borrowed from a 401k is out of your investments until you pay it back.

Common questions

Is a 401k loan safer than a HELOC?+
It is safer for your home, because your home is not collateral. It is riskier for your retirement, because the borrowed money is out of your investments, and a balance you do not repay is generally taxed as a distribution.
How much can I borrow from my 401k?+
The IRS generally limits plan loans to the lesser of 50,000 dollars or 50 percent of your vested account balance. Some plans allow up to 10,000 dollars when half your balance is less than that, and plans are not required to offer loans at all.
What happens to a 401k loan if I leave my job?+
Your plan may require you to repay the full balance. If you do not, the unpaid amount is generally treated as a taxable distribution, though you may be able to avoid immediate tax by rolling it over by your tax return due date, including extensions.
