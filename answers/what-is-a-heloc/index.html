What is a HELOC?

Written by Home Equity Lending. Last updated November 23, 2026.

A HELOC, or home equity line of credit, is a line of credit secured by your home. A lender sets a credit limit, you can draw money as you need it up to that limit during the draw period, and you repay what you borrow with interest.

Because your home is the collateral, you could lose your home if you cannot repay. Your home equity is not spare cash: it turns into money only if you sell the home or borrow against it.

How a HELOC works

  • Your equity is your home's value minus what you owe on every loan it secures. See How much equity do I have in my home?
  • Your credit limit is usually smaller than your equity. Lenders generally let you borrow only part of it, and each lender sets its own limits based on things like your home's value, what you owe, your credit and your income. See How much can I borrow with a HELOC?
  • During the draw period you can borrow, repay and borrow again up to your limit. When the draw period ends, the repayment period begins and you repay what you owe.
  • HELOCs usually have a variable interest rate, so your payment can change even if you borrow nothing new.
  • You do not need a paid off home. If you have a mortgage, a HELOC usually sits behind it as a second loan on the home, and your first mortgage stays in place.
  • When you sell your home, you are generally required to pay off the HELOC in full. See What happens to a HELOC when I sell my house?

Questions to ask yourself

  • Would I put my house behind this expense?
  • If my income dropped next year, could I still make the payments?
  • Could my budget handle a higher payment if the rate went up or the repayment period started?
  • Do I have emergency savings separate from this line?
  • How long will I stay in this home?
  • Have I compared other options, such as a home equity loan, a personal loan or savings?

Risks in plain language

  • Your home is the collateral. If you cannot repay, you could lose your home.
  • Payments can change. HELOCs usually have a variable interest rate, so the payment can move even if you borrow nothing new.
  • Payments can jump at repayment. After the draw period ends, payments can be much higher, and some plans require a large balloon payment.
  • The lender can freeze or reduce the line, for example if your home's value falls a lot.
  • If you use a HELOC to pay off credit cards or other unsecured debt, it turns unsecured debt into debt secured by your home.
  • When you sell, you generally have to pay off the HELOC in full, so you keep less from the sale.

Common questions

Is a HELOC a second mortgage?
If you already have a mortgage, a HELOC is usually a second loan secured by your home, behind your first mortgage. Your first mortgage stays in place.
Do I need to pay off my mortgage to get a HELOC?
No. A HELOC usually sits behind your existing mortgage. What matters is how much equity you have, along with things like your credit, income and debts, and each lender sets its own rules.
Can I borrow all of my equity?
Usually not. Lenders generally let you borrow only part of your equity, and each lender sets its own limits, so your equity and your credit limit are two different numbers.
What is the difference between a HELOC and a home equity loan?
A home equity loan usually pays one lump sum that you repay on a set schedule, usually at a fixed rate. A HELOC is usually a line of credit you draw from as needed, usually at a variable rate. Both are secured by your home.
Is a HELOC a government program?
No. A HELOC is a loan from a lender, such as a bank or credit union, not a government benefit. You repay what you borrow, and your home secures the line.
Is HELOC interest tax deductible?
IRS Publication 936 says interest on a home equity line is deductible as home mortgage interest only when the money buys, builds or substantially improves the home that secures it, and only if you itemize. Ask a tax professional about your situation.
Can I cancel a HELOC after I sign?
On your main home, federal law generally gives you three business days to cancel a home equity line or loan in writing, counted from when you sign and receive the required disclosures and notices.
