How much have home prices risen since you bought? By state, 2000 to 2026

Written by Home Equity Lending. Last updated November 18, 2026.

If you bought a home in mid 2015, prices for comparable U.S. homes have risen about 105.8% since then, roughly doubling, according to our calculations from FHFA's House Price Index through the second quarter of 2026. For a purchase in mid 2020 the rise is 56.6%, and for mid 2023 it is 11.3%.

The typical owner living in their home today moved in during 2013, according to the Census Bureau, and U.S. prices have risen 126.5% since mid 2013. How much your own home has gained depends on where it is and when you bought.

What the numbers show

  • The longer you have owned, the larger the rise. U.S. prices are up 214.0% since mid 2000, 134.4% since mid 2010 and 11.3% since mid 2023.
  • Since mid 2015, prices rose the most in Idaho (176.5%), Utah (139.7%) and Florida (138.4%), and the least in North Dakota (57.9%), Louisiana (46.6%) and the District of Columbia (40.8%).
  • Recent buyers have seen much smaller gains. Since mid 2023, prices rose 3.8% in Texas and 1.9% in Colorado, and were about flat in the District of Columbia (−0.1%).
  • In some states, people who bought just before the 2006 peak have gained less than other long time owners. Since mid 2005, prices are up 56.7% in Maryland, 57.8% in California and 70.1% in Nevada, compared with 111.9% nationally.
  • Prices fell over the past year in four states: California, Colorado, New Mexico and Washington.
  • As an illustration only, a home bought for $300,000 in mid 2015 that rose with the national index would be worth about $617,000 today. Your home's value, and your equity, depend on your own home and what you owe on it.

A HELOC or home equity loan uses your home as collateral. If you cannot repay, you could lose your home.

The figures on this page describe home prices across each state. They do not tell you what your home is worth or how much you could borrow, and they are not an offer of credit.

How we built this

  • Home prices are FHFA's seasonally adjusted, purchase only House Price Index for each state and for the United States. The index tracks repeat sales of the same single family homes with mortgages backed by Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac, so it measures how prices of comparable homes changed, not the value of any one home.
  • For each purchase year we compare the index in the second quarter of 2026, the latest quarter published, with the index in the second quarter of that year. The rise is the 2026 index divided by the purchase year index, minus one, shown as a percent. Using the same quarter each year keeps the comparison even.
  • We checked our method against FHFA's own published 1 year and 5 year state changes. Our figures match FHFA's for all 50 states and the District of Columbia.
  • The index is not adjusted for inflation, so the figures are price rises in dollars of each year.
  • FHFA flags states with fewer than 15,000 sales over the latest ten years, where the index can move more from quarter to quarter: Alaska, Delaware, the District of Columbia, Hawaii, Vermont, West Virginia and Wyoming. They are marked with an asterisk.
  • The year the typical owner moved in is the Census Bureau's median year the householder moved into an owner occupied home, from table B25039 of the 2025 American Community Survey 1 year estimates. It is a survey estimate with a margin of error of one or two years, given in the CSV. It describes owners living in their homes now, not everyone who ever bought.
  • The CSV gives the rise for every purchase year from 2000 to 2025 for each state and the United States. Puerto Rico is not included.
  • FHFA plans to publish third quarter 2026 data on November 24, 2026. We will update this page after that release.

Risks in plain language

  • A HELOC or home equity loan is secured by your home. If you cannot repay, you could lose your home.
  • Rising prices do not lock in your equity. Home values can fall, as they did over the past year in four states, and they fell sharply in many states after 2006.
  • If your home loses value, a lender may freeze or lower a home equity line.
  • Borrowing against equity turns part of that equity back into debt.
  • These are index figures for each state as a whole. Your home may have risen more or less than its state, and only an appraisal or a lender can value your home.

Common questions

How much have home prices gone up since 2020?
U.S. home prices rose 56.6% from the second quarter of 2020 to the second quarter of 2026, according to our calculations from FHFA's House Price Index. By state, the rise ranges from 11.9% in the District of Columbia to 85.1% in Vermont.
How much have home prices gone up in the last 10 years?
From mid 2016 to mid 2026, U.S. home prices rose 95.3%, according to our calculations from FHFA's House Price Index. The largest rise was in Idaho (158.9%) and the smallest in the District of Columbia (31.6%).
How can I estimate how much my home has gone up since I bought it?
Find your state and the year you bought in the table, the calculator on this page or the CSV, then apply that percent to what you paid. For example, a 50% rise on a $200,000 purchase points to about $300,000. This is a rough guide based on comparable homes in your state. Your home's actual value depends on its location, condition and changes since you bought, and only an appraisal can tell you what it is worth.
Does a higher home value mean I have more equity?
Equity is what your home is worth minus what you owe on it. A rise in value adds to your equity only if your loan balance does not grow by as much. A lender decides how much, if anything, a homeowner can borrow, and any home equity loan or line uses the home as collateral.
Are home prices still going up?
In most states, prices were still rising as of mid 2026, according to our calculations from FHFA's House Price Index. From the second quarter of 2025 to the second quarter of 2026, the index rose in 47 of 51 states counting the District of Columbia, and fell in California, Colorado, New Mexico and Washington. Prices can fall, and past rises do not tell you what prices will do next.
When did the typical homeowner move in?
The typical owner living in their home in 2025 moved in during 2013, according to the Census Bureau's American Community Survey. By state, the median ranges from 2009 in Hawaii to 2016 in Nevada.
